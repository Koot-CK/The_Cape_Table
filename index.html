Soft Sweet Vetkoek & Old-School Sweet Curry Mince

Equipment

Prep — Mise en Place

Get all of this ready before you start cooking. It isn’t repeated in the steps below — they just refer back to “from Prep”.

Vetkoek Dough

1
Weigh the dry goods
500 g cake flour, 6 g salt, 25 g sugar, 5 g instant yeast — weighed out and ready by the bowl.
2
Soften the butter
30 g salted butter, left at room temperature until soft enough to work into the dough by hand.
3
Warm the yeast liquid
Combine 250 ml milk and 250 ml water in a jug and warm to about 35–36°C — warm, not hot. Stir in the yeast and a pinch of the sugar. Rest 5–10 minutes until slightly foamy.
4
Sift the dry mix — three times
Sift the flour, salt and remaining sugar together into the mixing bowl. Sift twice more, tipping it back in each time. Breaks up lumps and spreads the salt and sugar evenly.
5
Set up the frying station
Pot with 1.5–2 litres sunflower oil, thermometer clipped to the side, wire rack lined with paper towel, slotted spoon and tongs within reach.

Mince

1
Prep the mince aromatics
Finely chop 1 onion and crush 2 garlic cloves. Weigh out the 500 g beef mince.
2
Measure the mince seasonings & sauces
30 ml macadamia oil, 6 g salt, a pinch (~0.5 g) white pepper, 15 ml Worcestershire sauce, 1 tin (400 g) chopped tomatoes, 15 g curry powder, 4 g ground coriander, 0.5 g nutmeg, 1 g five spice, 1 g turmeric (keep this one separate — it goes in late), 60 g apricot chutney, 40 g apricot jam, 15 ml brown vinegar, and a small splash (~30 ml) of water for loosening the pan.
3
Mix the gravy paste
Stir 15 g Bisto gravy powder into about 30 ml cold water until smooth. Set aside.

Cooking Instructions

Vetkoek Dough

1
Add the liquid in three additions
Split the warmed yeast liquid (from Prep) into three portions. Mix the first third into the sifted flour mix (from Prep), then the second third. Add the final third gradually, stopping once the dough is a soft, cohesive ball — not sticky-wet, not stiff and dry.
2
Add butter and knead — stretch and fold
Work in the softened butter (from Prep). On a lightly floured surface, with lightly oiled hands, stretch a section of dough away from you, fold it back over, quarter-turn, repeat. Knead 10–12 minutes until smooth, soft and elastic.
3
First rise — the most important step
Cover and, if the room is colder than 28°C, place inside the black plastic bag somewhere warm and sunny (check on it — hot sun can overshoot 35–36°C). Leave about 1 hour. Doubled early? Move on right away. Not doubled at an hour? Keep checking every 15 minutes until it is — don't rush the rise.
4
Divide into 8 equal portions
Roll the risen dough into one long, even log. Cut into 8 equal sections with a bench scraper — this gives far more even portions than shaping balls one by one. Check a couple on the scale.
5
Shape and rest
Shape each portion into a smooth ball with oiled hands. Cover loosely, rest 15–20 minutes. Cooled down before frying? A few minutes back in the black bag brings it back up.
6
Flatten
Gently flatten each ball to about 1.5–2 cm thick. Don't press too thin — it needs room to rise and stay soft in the middle.

Frying

1
Heat the oil to 165–170°C
The "borrel" test: drop in a small offcut of dough — it should rise and bubble immediately. Sinks or barely bubbles → too cool. Colours in seconds → too hot. Never let it drop below 140°C during frying, or the vetkoek will turn greasy.
2
Fry in small batches
Only 2–3 vetkoek at a time, depending on pot size. Do not overcrowd — it drops the oil temperature and fries unevenly.
3
Fry 4–5 minutes per side
Turn once. Fry until deep golden brown all over and cooked through in the centre.
4
Drain and recover oil temperature
Lift out onto the paper-towel-lined rack. Let the oil come back to 165–170°C before the next batch.

What a properly fried vetkoek looks like

Outside: an even, deep golden-amber brown all over — not pale, not dark or blistered. Inside, cut open: soft and slightly springy, with a network of medium-sized air holes through the crumb — not tight and dense, not a few large irregular holes. That even, medium-holed crumb tells you the rise, hydration and fry temperature all landed correctly.

Old-School Sweet Curry Mince

1
Soften the onion
Heat the macadamia oil (from Prep) in the pan over medium heat (roughly 150°C) and fry the chopped onion (from Prep) until soft and translucent — no spices yet. Add the crushed garlic (from Prep) for the final 30 seconds.
2
Loosen the pan
Add the splash of water (about 30 ml, from Prep), stirring to lift anything stuck to the base. Makes the mince easier to break up once it goes in.
3
Brown the mince — lightly
Medium-high heat (roughly 180°C). Add the 500 g beef mince (from Prep) and break it up as it cooks. Just lose the raw colour, lightly coloured all over — not deeply browned. It builds full flavour during the simmer ahead.
4
Build the sauce base Taste
Add the 6 g salt, ~0.5 g white pepper, 15 ml Worcestershire sauce and the tin (400 g) chopped tomatoes (all from Prep). Stir through thoroughly, then taste — this is your base flavour before any spice or acid.
5
Add the spices Taste
Stir in the 15 g curry powder, 4 g coriander, 0.5 g nutmeg and 1 g five spice (from Prep) — hold back the turmeric. Cook a minute or two, then taste again: nutmeg and five spice are both potent and easy to overshoot. Adjust, then simmer gently 15–20 minutes total until tender.
6
Add the vinegar Taste
Stir in the 15 ml brown vinegar (from Prep), simmer a minute or two, then taste a third time — this shows the sweet-versus-acid balance before any sweetness goes in.
7
Sweeten
Stir in the 60 g apricot chutney and 40 g apricot jam (from Prep). Simmer a further 5 minutes to let the sweetness settle in.
8
Add the turmeric — late
In the final 2–3 minutes, stir in the 1 g turmeric set aside in Prep — keeps its colour bright and its flavour clean rather than dulled by a long simmer.
9
Thicken
Stir in the gravy paste mixed in Prep (15 g Bisto gravy powder + 30 ml water), simmer 2–3 minutes to a thick, scoopable consistency — not runny, not so dry it falls apart when spooned.

To Serve

✓
Fill and serve
Slice each vetkoek open, not all the way through, and fill generously with the warm sweet curry mince.

Flavour Wheel

Balancing the Flavour

Three tasting points are built into the method — after the base, after the spices, after the vinegar — so you can catch and correct the balance before it's locked in by the sweetness and thickener.

The core rule: every sweet element needs an acid to answer it. Chutney and jam bring sweetness; brown vinegar cuts through it so the dish doesn't taste flat or cloying. Too sweet → more vinegar. Too sharp → a touch more chutney or jam.

Salt sharpens every other flavour — a muted mince is often missing salt, not another ingredient. White pepper sits with the salt for background warmth, without black pepper's more forward, grassy heat or dark flecks.

Umami from the browned mince, tomatoes and gravy powder gives body and richness — part of why the mince is only lightly browned early, since its full savoury depth builds during the simmer.

Aromatic warmth (curry powder, five spice, nutmeg, coriander) should stay in the background. One spice jumping out usually means it went in too early or too heavy-handed relative to the others.

Tips

Notes on Technique

Liquid no hotter than 35–36°C: yeast weakens well before boiling point, with danger territory from the mid-40s°C and full kill-off around 60°C. 35–36°C, close to body temperature, sits safely inside the active range — warm, not hot, to the touch.

Oil never below 140°C: below that, the crust takes too long to set and seal, so the dough absorbs noticeably more oil before it's sealed. Work in the 165–170°C range and treat 140°C strictly as a floor.

Three-times sifting: breaks up lumps and spreads the salt and sugar evenly through the flour, for more even fermentation.

Light-browning the mince: lean mince in small pieces dries out and toughens fast under high heat. It simmers for 15–20 minutes afterward anyway, so there's no need to drive off extra moisture upfront.

The black plastic bag: keeps dough warmer than the surrounding air on a cool day. On a hot, sunny day it can run past the target temperature — check on it rather than leaving it for a long stretch.

Baked Sago Pudding with Apricot Jam & Meringue

Yield: 6 portions ★★★★☆ 4/5 Hard

Equipment

Prep — Mise en Place

Get all of this ready before you start cooking. It isn’t repeated in the steps below — they just refer back to “from Prep”.

Get Ready

1
Soak the sago
Rinse 140 g sago pearls under cold water, then cover with cold water and soak for 1 hour. Drain well through a fine sieve. Don’t skip this — unsoaked sago cooks unevenly, with a harder centre in some pearls. Start this first — everything else can happen while it soaks.
2
Weigh the custard base
1000 ml (1 litre) full-cream milk, 50 g white sugar, 55 g salted butter (cubed), 1.5 g salt — measured out and ready by the stove.
3
Separate the eggs
4 large eggs: yolks into a bowl with the 5 ml vanilla extract whisked in; whites into a separate, grease-free bowl, reserved for the meringue.
4
Butter the dish and stage the bain-marie
Butter 1 large oven dish (or 6 individual ramekins). Have the roasting pan and a kettle of water ready for the bain-marie at bake time.
5
Preheat the oven
Preheat to 160°C ahead of the first bake.
6
Weigh the jam & meringue topping
180 g apricot jam, 10 g cornflour, 2 g cream of tartar, 200 g caster sugar.

Cooking Instructions

Sago Custard Base

1
Build the milk base
Warm the milk (from Prep) in the saucepan over medium heat. Add the sugar, butter and salt (from Prep), stirring until the butter melts and the sugar dissolves. Bring to a gentle simmer — not a rolling boil.
2
Cook the sago until fully translucent Check
Stir in the drained sago (from Prep). Reduce to low heat and simmer gently, stirring regularly so it doesn’t catch on the base of the pot, until every pearl has turned from opaque white to fully translucent — usually 15–20 minutes. Bite one: there should be no hard white speck at the centre. This takes longer than feels natural; rushing it at higher heat scorches the pot without finishing the pearls.
3
Temper in the egg yolks
Remove from the heat and cool for about 5 minutes. Take the egg yolks prepped with vanilla (from Prep), then whisk in a ladleful of the warm sago mixture to bring them up to temperature gradually. Stir this tempered mixture back into the main pot, off the heat, until fully combined.
4
First bake — set the custard Check
Pour into the buttered dish (from Prep) and set inside the roasting pan. Pour hot water from the kettle into the roasting pan until it comes about halfway up the sides of the pudding dish. Bake at the preheated 160°C for 30 minutes (large dish) or about 20 minutes (ramekins). Check doneness two ways: it should give a gentle, even wobble across the centre when nudged (not sunken-liquid, not fully firm), and an instant-read thermometer at the centre should read roughly 82–85°C. Sago's cloudiness can hide the wobble, so if it hits temperature before it looks set, trust the thermometer. It keeps setting from residual heat and through the second bake, so pulling it here rather than at fully firm is what protects it from turning rubbery.

Jam, Meringue & Second Bake

5
Warm and spread the jam
While the base bakes, warm the apricot jam (from Prep) slightly — a few seconds in the microwave or a small pot — just until it loosens enough to spread easily. Don’t let it boil. Spread evenly over the warm baked custard.
6
Dust with cornflour
Very lightly dust the jam layer with the cornflour (from Prep) through a small sieve. This thin, barely-visible layer stops the meringue from weeping syrup where it sits against the moist jam, and helps it hold its shape during the second bake.
7
Whip the meringue
Whisk the reserved egg whites (from Prep) with the cream of tartar (from Prep) until foamy, then add the caster sugar (from Prep) gradually, a spoonful at a time, whisking continuously until stiff, glossy, and holding a firm peak on the whisk.
8
Top and seal
Pile or pipe the meringue over the jam layer, spreading it right to the edges of the dish so it seals the jam layer completely. Any exposed jam will bubble and scorch in the second bake.
9
Second bake — set the meringue Check
Return to the 160°C oven (no water bath needed) for 15–20 minutes, until the meringue peaks are golden brown and set on the outside but still soft within. A cook’s blowtorch over the piped peaks is a faster alternative if you’d rather not run the oven again — good for ramekins plated to order.

What a properly finished bake looks like

Meringue peaks deep golden brown, crisp outside and soft within. Custard set at the edges with a gentle give in the centre when spooned — not runny, not rubbery.

Variation: Meringue Skin Topping (optional)

Instead of the piped, spongy meringue above, you can finish with a thin glossy skin over the jam. Three changes from the method: far less sugar, softer peaks, and a thin spread instead of a piped mound.

Whisk the 4 reserved egg whites with a pinch of cream of tartar until foamy, then add only 20–30 g caster sugar — just enough to stabilise the foam, not to build volume. Whip to soft-to-medium peaks only, then spread thinly (2–4 mm) right to the edges to seal the jam, rather than piling or piping. Bake as before but check earlier — a thin layer sets in about 8–10 minutes rather than 15–20 — or finish with a blowtorch instead of a second oven bake for a closer-to-true skin.

This version is more fragile than the full meringue, so lean harder on the cornflour dusting to stop it weeping against the jam, and serve it fairly soon after finishing rather than making it well ahead.

Rest & Serve

10
Rest before serving
Let the pudding stand for 5–10 minutes out of the oven before serving. This lets the custard finish settling so it holds together cleanly when spooned, rather than running.
11
To serve
Serve warm, spooned straight from the dish. For individually plated service, torch the meringue on each ramekin to order and serve immediately while the meringue is still warm and the custard beneath is soft.

Flavour Wheel

Balancing the Flavour

Every rich, milky element here needs a tart note to answer it, or the dish reads as flat and one-note — the same principle as the curry mince. The apricot jam’s tartness is what cuts through the sweetness of the custard and meringue; without it, three layers of sweet-on-sweet-on-sweet turns cloying fast. If the finished pudding tastes flat, it’s rarely a sugar problem — usually the jam layer is too thin, or too sweet a jam was used instead of a properly tart apricot one.

Salt in the custard isn’t there to taste salty — at 1.5 g it sharpens the milk and vanilla underneath the sugar. Skipping it makes the custard taste duller, not just less salty.

Vanilla should sit in the background, rounding out the milk flavour rather than announcing itself. If it’s noticeable as its own flavour, it’s been overdosed.

Texture matters as much as taste: soft custard, a thin jammy layer, and a crisp-then-soft meringue crust are three different textures in one spoonful. Losing any one of the three — an unset custard, a jam layer melted into the custard, or an over-baked, chalky meringue — flattens that contrast.

Tips

Notes on Technique

Weigh the sago and other measured ingredients in grams: a cup or ml of sago, jam or sugar packs differently depending how firmly it’s scooped or how warm the jam is. Weighing removes that variable.

The sago must go fully translucent before anything else: an opaque centre means unhydrated starch that will never fully soften in the oven bake that follows. This has to be solved on the stovetop, not in the oven.

Temper the yolks, don’t stir them straight in: egg yolks scramble around 70–80°C. Warming them gradually with a ladleful of the hot mixture first lets them thicken the custard instead of curdling into visible flecks.

The bain-marie for the first bake: direct oven heat cooks the outside of an egg-thickened custard much faster than the centre, giving a rubbery edge and a curdled texture. The water bath moderates the heat so the whole custard sets gently and evenly.

Pull it at “still wobbling gently”, not fully firm: the custard keeps cooking from residual heat once out of the oven, and again through the second bake. Baking to fully firm at this stage all but guarantees an overcooked, rubbery result by the end.

Dust the jam with cornflour before the meringue: meringue piped onto a moist, sugary surface tends to weep syrup underneath as it bakes, or slide off. A thin cornflour dusting absorbs that surface moisture and gives the meringue something drier to grip.

Cream of tartar in the meringue: a mild acid that stabilises the whipped egg-white proteins, giving a meringue that holds stiff peaks longer and is less likely to weep or deflate before it reaches the oven.

Add the caster sugar gradually: whipping it in a spoonful at a time lets it fully dissolve into the whites as they whip, giving a glossy, stable meringue rather than a grainy one that weeps sugar syrup during baking.

Airfried Karoo Lamb Tjops — “Braaitjops” Style

Equipment

Prep — Mise en Place

Get all of this ready before any heat goes near the air fryer. It isn’t repeated in the steps below — they just refer back to “from Prep”.

Get Ready

1
Vinegar wash
Combine the 250 ml vinegar (from Shop) with 1.5 L water in the large bowl. Submerge the chops, turning once halfway through. After 15 minutes, remove, rinse briefly under cold running water, and pat completely dry with paper towel — wet meat won’t take a rub or render fat cleanly later.
2
Dry brine
Season both sides of each washed, dried chop with 20 g of the coarse salt (from Shop). Set uncovered on a rack over a tray in the fridge for 1–24 hours. Short on time? 40 minutes at room temperature is the minimum.
3
Toast & smoke the spices
Toast the 8 g coriander seeds and 3 cloves in a dry pan over medium heat for 1–2 minutes, until fragrant and just starting to pop — don’t let them scorch. Line the wok with foil, pile the smoking material in the centre, and set the wire rack above it. Spread the toasted coriander and cloves across the rack along with the 6 g paprika, 4 g garlic powder and 4 g onion powder, then cover tightly with the lid. Place over medium heat until you see the first wisp of smoke escape, then seal any gaps and drop to low. Hold for 6–8 minutes, then turn off the heat and leave sealed a further 5 minutes before opening — do this near an open window or extractor fan, it’s a strong smell the first time through.
4
Mix the rub
Grind the smoked coriander, cloves, paprika, garlic powder and onion powder to a fine consistency. Stir in the remaining 6 g coarse salt, 3 g black pepper, 2 g white pepper, the ¼ tsp freshly grated nutmeg, and the ¼ tsp MSG if using. These four go in after smoking, not before, so the pepper stays sharp and the nutmeg keeps its top notes. Mix thoroughly until even in colour — this is the finished rub, referred to simply as “the rub” from here on.
5
Prep the butter
Cut the 40 g butter (from Shop) into 8 knobs, about 5 g each. Keep chilled until needed.
6
Apply the rub & rig the chops
Once the chops have finished brining, pat off any surface moisture and coat both sides generously with the rub, pressing it on. Thread the skewer through the tail flap of each chop, linking all 8 into a self-supporting standing ring — bone edge down, fat cap facing up and outward. Leave enough spacing between chops for air to move freely. This ring is what goes into the fryer — cooking starts from here.

Cooking Instructions

Full Timing Schedule, Start to Plate

Vinegar wash — 15 min

Dry brine — 1 to 24 hr, minimum 40 min (passive — happens ahead of time, not counted in the active time below)

Toast & smoke the spices — 15 min

Mix the rub — 2 min

Butter, cut into knobs — 1 min

Apply the rub & rig the chops — 5 min

Preheat the Philips XL — 3 min

Air fry, fat-up — 9 min

Butter finish, still in the fryer — 3 min (12 min total air-fry time)

Flame finish on the ring — 2 to 3 min

Rest — 5 min

Total active time, vinegar wash to plate: roughly 60 minutes (add the dry brine on top — 40 min minimum, or leave it overnight and the rest runs in about an hour on the day)

Air Fryer

1
Preheat
Preheat the Philips XL to 200°C for 3 minutes, basket empty.
2
Air fry
Lower the standing ring in (from Prep), bone-side down, fat caps toward the top of the basket where the fan and element sit. Air fry for 9 minutes.
3
Butter finish Check
Open the basket and place a knob of butter (from Prep) on the fat cap of each chop. Close and air fry a further 3 minutes — total air-fry time 12 minutes, the tested ceiling for this thickness. Check internal temperature reaches at least 60°C.

Flame Finish, Rest & Serve

4
Flame finish Check
Lift the whole ring out onto the heatproof board — it will be very hot, and the fat should already be rendered and glossy. Have the torch lit and ready before this point so the chops don’t sit and cool while you find it. Working around the ring, torch each fat cap and face for 10–15 seconds, rotating the ring as you go. Two or three passes around the whole ring is usually enough — you’re looking for blistering and a char that reads as coal-fire, not just browning. No torch? See the Tips tab for broiler and cast-iron alternatives.
5
Rest
Unthread the chops carefully. Rest 5 minutes on a warm plate, loosely tented with foil — this matters more here than usual, since the meat has taken heat from both the fryer and direct flame.
6
Serve
Serve straight, or with pap en sous, a tomato-and-onion smoor, chakalaka, or a wedge of lemon and extra flaky salt.

Flavour Wheel

Balancing the Flavour

This rub doesn’t lean on sugar or a wet baste, so the balance point is different from a basted braai — there’s no sweet element to answer with acid. Smoke and char, answered by the vinegar wash and whatever’s fresh on the plate, is the primary axis: the stovetop smoke and the torch finish are both doing real work, and pushed too far either one can tip the chop toward bitter or ashy rather than braai-like. If a test batch reads as too heavy on the smoke, shorten the stovetop smoking hold (try 5 minutes instead of 6–8) before touching the torch step.

Salt sharpens rather than just seasons — it’s doing double duty here, once in the dry brine, once in the rub, so if a finished chop tastes muted, the fix is more likely to be a longer or more thorough brine than more rub.

Aromatic warmth (nutmeg, cloves, coriander, pepper) stays in the background. Nutmeg and cloves are both potent in small amounts, which is why they’re measured in fractions of a teaspoon rather than whole ones. If either one is jumping out on tasting, it’s overshooting — cut it back before adjusting anything else.

Tips

Notes on Technique

Why the vinegar wash comes before the dry brine, not instead of it: the wash is about cleaning the surface of store-bought meat, not seasoning it. Salt needs a clean, dry surface to do its job properly, so wash and dry fully before the salt ever goes on.

Why the nutmeg and MSG go in after smoking: both are delicate or easily overwhelmed by prolonged heat and smoke exposure. Grating the nutmeg in at the end keeps its aromatics bright rather than dulled.

Why fat-cap-up, bone-down in the Philips XL specifically: this model’s fan and heating element sit above a single static basket. Standing the chops with the fat cap toward that airflow gives the rendering fat a clear path to run back down over the meat as it melts — the closest thing to self-basting this fryer can do without a rotisserie attachment.

Why the torch happens on the ring, not chop by chop: taking each chop off the skewer to torch it individually means the first ones finish and start cooling before the last ones are even off the fryer. Torching the whole standing ring in place keeps them together so they all go into the rest at roughly the same temperature.

Why the 12-minute air-fry ceiling holds even with the butter step folded in: that number came from your own testing on this chop thickness — the butter’s final 3 minutes is inside that ceiling, not added on top of it.

The lamb itself: real Karoo lamb already carries a herby, mineral flavour from grazing on renosterbos and other veld scrub — it’s the reason it’s prized. Keep the rub restrained and skip an acidic overnight marinade; a wet marinade sitting on raw lamb overnight softens the texture instead of seasoning it. Salt early — there’s no wet sauce added late in this version; the rendered fat and the final butter knob do that job instead.

Wood chips, why not in the fryer: a foil pouch of soaked chips works in an oven with a rack above the element, but not in a basket-style air fryer — too close to the fan and heating coil to be safe. That’s why the smoke here is built into the rub on the stovetop beforehand, rather than attempted inside the fryer itself.

No torch? Alternative finish methods: the tested method uses a blowtorch on the standing ring, which gives the most control and doesn’t require unthreading the chops first. Two stand-ins work if a torch isn’t on hand, though both need the chops taken off the skewer and laid flat first: a broiler/oven grill — 60–90 seconds per side under a very hot grill element, chops close to the heat; or a dry cast-iron pan — screaming-hot, no oil, 30–45 seconds a side, pressed down once. Either goes in the same place the torch step sits above, just before resting — expect a slightly less even char than the torch gives.

Time & temperature reference, flat-lay, no rig: the tested recipe above is built specifically around the standing rig and its fixed 12-minute sequence for 20–25mm chops — don’t mix that number with the table below, which is for cooking the same cut of chop flat in the basket, without the rig, useful for troubleshooting.

Cape Table White Burger Sauce (SA McDonald’s-Style)

Yield: ~300g (18–20 tbsp) Trial recipe — not yet rated Easy

Equipment

Prep — Mise en Place

Get all of this ready before combining — there’s no heat in this recipe, but the order still matters. It isn’t repeated in the steps below — they just refer back to “from Prep”.

Get Ready

1
Mince the gherkins — finely
Pulse the 30 g gherkins (from Shop) in a mini chopper, or mince them by hand as finely as you can, until they’re close to a paste with no visible chunks. This is the detail that keeps the sauce smooth and pale rather than flecked and chunky.
2
Measure the dry seasoning
In a small bowl, combine the 3 g onion powder, 3 g garlic powder, 5 g sugar, 2 g salt, 1 g white pepper, and the 1 g MSG if using (all from Shop), so they’re ready to go in together. This is the finished seasoning mix, referred to simply as “the dry seasoning” from here on.

Cooking Instructions

Full Timing Schedule, Start to Plate

Mince the gherkins — 3 min

Measure the dry seasoning — 2 min

Whisk the mayonnaise and mustard together — 1 min

Bring it together — 2 min

Taste & adjust — 2 min

Rest in the fridge — minimum 30 min, best 2 hr or overnight (passive — happens ahead of time, not counted in the active time below)

Total active time, start to jar: roughly 10 minutes (add the rest on top — 30 min minimum, best done well ahead)

Assemble & Rest

1
Combine the base
In the mixing bowl, whisk the 200 g mayonnaise and the 15 g mustard (both from Shop) together until smooth and even in colour.
2
Bring it together
Stir in the minced gherkins and the dry seasoning (both from Prep), along with the 10 ml vinegar (from Shop). Whisk thoroughly until fully combined and uniform in colour — you shouldn’t see streaks of mustard or pockets of seasoning.
3
Taste and adjust
Taste the sauce. More vinegar sharpens it, more sugar rounds it out, more salt lifts a sauce that tastes muted. This is the point to dial it in to your own taste.
4
Rest
Transfer to an airtight jar and refrigerate for at least 30 minutes before using. For the best result, give it 2 hours or, better, overnight — this is what lets the onion and garlic powders fully hydrate and the flavours properly meld.
5
Serve
Spoon generously onto the bun before the patty and lettuce go on, or use as a dip for chips or nuggets. Keeps refrigerated in an airtight container for up to 1 week.

Flavour Wheel

Balancing the Flavour

The core rule here is the same one that runs through the rest of the Cape Table kitchen: every sweet element needs an acid to answer it. The sugar rounds the sauce out; the vinegar (and the gherkin’s own brine) cuts through it so the finished sauce doesn’t taste flat or one-note. If it tastes too sweet, add a touch more vinegar. If it’s too sharp, a little more sugar brings it back.

Salt (and the optional MSG) sharpens every other flavour in the sauce rather than just adding saltiness. A sauce that tastes dull or muted after balancing sweet and sour is almost always under-salted, not missing another ingredient.

Aromatic warmth (onion powder, garlic powder, mustard) should sit in the background, giving the sauce its familiar “fast food” character without any one of the three jumping out. Why there’s no paprika: the internationally known “official” Big Mac sauce recipe leans on paprika and a coarser relish for its pink-orange colour and visible fleck. Leaving the paprika out, and mincing the gherkins to a near-paste instead of leaving them chunky, is what keeps this version pale and smooth — matching the white mayo-style sauce served locally rather than the orange US version.

Tips

Notes on Technique

Why the gherkins are minced so fine: visible relish chunks are what give most copycat recipes their flecked, chunky look. Blitzing them to a near-paste is the single biggest thing that makes this taste and look like the smooth white sauce rather than a Thousand Island-style dressing.

Why real mayonnaise, not salad dressing: salad dressing is thinner and more vinegary out of the jar, which throws off both the texture and the sweet/acid balance built into this recipe. A full-egg mayonnaise gives the right body and a proper pale ivory colour.

Why the rest isn’t optional: tasted straight after mixing, the sauce will taste sharper and more “separate” — you can pick out the mustard, the vinegar, the onion powder as individual notes. After a few hours in the fridge, those flavours settle into each other and round out into one sauce.

MSG — optional, not essential: it adds a small savoury lift that’s part of why fast-food sauces taste the way they do, but the sauce works fine without it. Leave it out if you’d rather not use it; a small pinch of extra salt covers most of the gap.

Food safety: this is a mayonnaise-based sauce — keep it refrigerated, use within a week, and don’t leave it out of the fridge for more than about 2 hours at a time.

Scaling up: this recipe doubles or triples cleanly with no change to method — useful if you’re making it ahead for a bigger burger night or a braai.

Flavour Wheel

Balancing the Flavour

The primary axis here is sweet against acid/bright: the honey and brown sugar push the bottled teriyaki toward sticky and sweet, while the sauce’s own vinegar or mirin tang is what keeps the finished bites from tasting flat or cloying. If it tastes one-note sweet, a splash more of the bottled sauce itself (rather than more honey) usually fixes it, since that’s where the tang lives.

Salt (the seasoning and the teriyaki’s own soy base) sharpens every other flavour in the dish. Its opposite on the wheel isn’t more salt but the risk of over-caramelising — the glaze catching and turning bitter if the second air-fry runs even a minute or two past 4 minutes.

The third axis pairs aromatic sesame notes (sesame oil, sesame seeds, spring onion) against umami richness (the pork belly’s own fat, and the teriyaki’s soy umami). Neither fixes the other — a batch that’s all sesame and no fat tastes thin, and one that’s all fat and no sesame tastes flat.

Tips

Notes on Technique

Why the pork is air-fried plain before it’s glazed: brushing the sauce on raw pork and air-frying the whole time burns the sugar before the pork is even cooked through. Glazing only in the last few minutes is what keeps it sticky instead of burnt.

Cube size: cutting the pork belly a shade smaller than 2.5cm speeds up the first air-fry but risks drying out before the fat fully renders — 2.5cm is the sweet spot for this amount of time.

Skin-on alternative: skin-on pork belly can be used instead, but the skin needs its own crackling technique (scoring, a longer dry stage, higher final heat) — that’s a different, harder recipe. This version deliberately uses skinless belly to keep it Easy.

Bottled sauce varies: check the label — some brands are already quite salty (little to no extra salt needed in Prep) and some are already sweet (drop the brown sugar to 5 ml). Taste the mixed glaze from Prep before using it and adjust.

Variations on the bottled sauce: whisk any of these into the same glaze mixture in Prep — spicier, add 5 ml chilli flakes or a squeeze of sriracha; extra aromatic, add a grated garlic clove and 5 ml grated ginger; pineapple twist, swap the honey for 30 ml crushed pineapple or pineapple juice; extra sesame, add another 5 ml sesame oil and double the sesame seed garnish; lower sugar, if the bottled brand is already sweet, drop the brown sugar to 5 ml and taste before using.

Prefer to make the sauce from scratch? See the Cape Table Homemade Teriyaki Sauce recipe (Sauces & Condiments). Use 160 ml of it in place of the bottled sauce above, and skip this recipe’s honey and brown sugar entirely — the homemade version is already balanced sweet and sticky on its own.

Flavour Wheel

Balancing the Flavour

The primary axis is sweet against acid/brightness: the brown sugar and honey carry the sweetness, while the mirin’s gentle tang is what stops the sauce tasting flat and one-note sweet. Tips

Notes on Technique

1
Rinse the rice
Tip the 300 g rice (from Shop) into the sieve and rinse under cold running water, swirling with your hand, until the water runs clear (about 1–2 minutes). This washes off surface starch so the grains stay separate rather than clumping together.
2
Measure the stock
Measure the 450 ml stock (from Shop).

Cooking Instructions

Full Timing Schedule, Start to Plate

Rinse rice & measure stock — 5 min

Bring to the boil — 4–5 min

Simmer, covered — 15 min

Off-heat check, if needed — up to 5 min

Rest, covered — 10 min

Total time, start to plate: roughly 30 minutes (about 20 minutes of it hands-off)

Boil, Simmer & Rest

1
Bring to the boil
Pour the rinsed rice and the stock (both from Prep) into the saucepan. Set the induction plate to its highest setting, 1500W, and bring to a rolling boil, uncovered, about 4–5 minutes — watch it, as it can boil over quickly at this ratio.
2
Drop to a simmer
The moment it reaches a rolling boil, cover with the lid and immediately drop the induction plate to its lowest setting, 500W. This drop from 1500W to 500W is what turns the boil into a gentle simmer without scorching the rice on the base of the pan.
3
Simmer
Simmer, covered, at 500W for 15 minutes. Don’t lift the lid or stir during this time — every peek lets out steam the rice needs to finish evenly.
4
Check
After 15 minutes, switch the induction plate off. Tilt the pan slightly to check — if liquid is still visibly pooling, replace the lid and leave it on the switched-off plate for a further 3–5 minutes before moving on.
5
Rest
Move the pan off the heat (or leave it on the now switched-off induction plate) and rest, covered, for 10 minutes. This is what finishes the grains — don’t skip it even though there’s no heat left under it.
6
Fluff & serve
Remove the lid, stir in the 15 ml butter if using, and fluff with a fork, separating the grains rather than stirring hard.

Flavour Wheel

Balancing the Flavour

The primary axis here is umami against dryness/separation: the stock carries almost all of this dish’s flavour, and the rinse, the 1:1.5 ratio, and the full rest afterward are what keep the grains separate instead of turning gluey. The technique is really the point of this recipe as much as the flavour is.

Salt (already carried by the stock) is why added salt is a pinch at most, never a measured amount — taste the stock itself first. Its opposite here isn’t more salt but gumminess or over-starch, the risk if the rinse or the rest gets skipped.

The third axis pairs aroma (whatever the stock itself is built from — chicken, vegetable, or otherwise) against richness (the optional butter, stirred in only at the very end so it doesn’t dull the boil).

Tips

Notes on Technique

Stock brands vary: a well-salted store-bought stock usually needs no extra salt; a homemade or low-sodium stock may want the optional pinch added in Prep.

If your plate’s settings don’t hit exactly 500W or 1500W: use the lowest available setting for the simmer stage and the highest available setting for the boil stage — the relationship between the two settings matters more than the exact numbers.

Doubling the batch: for 600 g rice and 900 ml stock, keep the same wattage settings but extend the simmer stage to 18–20 minutes — a fuller pan takes a little longer for the steam to penetrate evenly.

Still a touch firm after resting? Stir in 30 ml hot stock or water, re-cover, and rest a further 5 minutes off the heat rather than returning it to direct heat, which risks scorching the base layer.
