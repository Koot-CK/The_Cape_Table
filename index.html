Soft Sweet Vetkoek & Old-School Sweet Curry Mince

Equipment

Prep — Mise en Place

Get all of this ready before you start cooking. It isn’t repeated in the steps below — they just refer back to “from Prep”.

Get Ready

1
Weigh the dry goods
500 g cake flour, 6 g salt, 25 g sugar, 5 g instant yeast — weighed out and ready by the bowl.
2
Soften the butter
30 g salted butter, left at room temperature until soft enough to work into the dough by hand.
3
Warm the yeast liquid
Combine 250 ml milk and 250 ml water in a jug and warm to about 35–36°C — warm, not hot. Stir in the yeast and a pinch of the sugar. Rest 5–10 minutes until slightly foamy.
4
Sift the dry mix — three times
Sift the flour, salt and remaining sugar together into the mixing bowl. Sift twice more, tipping it back in each time. Breaks up lumps and spreads the salt and sugar evenly.
5
Set up the frying station
Pot with 1.5–2 litres sunflower oil, thermometer clipped to the side, wire rack lined with paper towel, slotted spoon and tongs within reach.
6
Prep the mince aromatics
Finely chop 1 onion and crush 2 garlic cloves. Weigh out the 500 g beef mince.
7
Measure the mince seasonings & sauces
6 g salt, a pinch (~0.5 g) white pepper, 15 ml Worcestershire sauce, 1 tin (400 g) chopped tomatoes, 15 g curry powder, 4 g ground coriander, 0.5 g nutmeg, 1 g five spice, 1 g turmeric (keep this one separate — it goes in late), 60 g apricot chutney, 40 g apricot jam, 15 ml brown vinegar, and a small splash (~30 ml) of water for loosening the pan.
8
Mix the gravy paste
Stir 15 g Bisto gravy powder into about 30 ml cold water until smooth. Set aside.

Cooking Instructions

Vetkoek Dough

1
Add the liquid in three additions
Split the warmed yeast liquid (from Prep) into three portions. Mix the first third into the sifted flour mix (from Prep), then the second third. Add the final third gradually, stopping once the dough is a soft, cohesive ball — not sticky-wet, not stiff and dry.
2
Add butter and knead — stretch and fold
Work in the 30 g softened butter (from Prep). On a lightly floured surface, with lightly oiled hands, stretch a section of dough away from you, fold it back over, quarter-turn, repeat. Knead 10–12 minutes until smooth, soft and elastic.
3
First rise — the most important step
Cover and, if the room is colder than 28°C, place inside the black plastic bag somewhere warm and sunny (check on it — hot sun can overshoot 35–36°C). Leave about 1 hour. Doubled early? Move on right away. Not doubled at an hour? Keep checking every 15 minutes until it is — don't rush the rise.
4
Divide into 8 equal portions
Roll the risen dough into one long, even log. Cut into 8 equal sections with a bench scraper — this gives far more even portions than shaping balls one by one. Check a couple on the scale.
5
Shape and rest
Shape each portion into a smooth ball with oiled hands. Cover loosely, rest 15–20 minutes. Cooled down before frying? A few minutes back in the black bag brings it back up.
6
Flatten
Gently flatten each ball to about 1.5–2 cm thick. Don't press too thin — it needs room to rise and stay soft in the middle.

Frying

1
Heat the oil to 165–170°C
The "borrel" test: drop in a small offcut of dough — it should rise and bubble immediately. Sinks or barely bubbles → too cool. Colours in seconds → too hot. Never let it drop below 140°C during frying, or the vetkoek will turn greasy.
2
Fry in small batches
Only 2–3 vetkoek at a time, depending on pot size. Do not overcrowd — it drops the oil temperature and fries unevenly.
3
Fry 4–5 minutes per side
Turn once. Fry until deep golden brown all over and cooked through in the centre.
4
Drain and recover oil temperature
Lift out onto the paper-towel-lined rack. Let the oil come back to 165–170°C before the next batch.

What a properly fried vetkoek looks like

Outside: an even, deep golden-amber brown all over — not pale, not dark or blistered. Inside, cut open: soft and slightly springy, with a network of medium-sized air holes through the crumb — not tight and dense, not a few large irregular holes. That even, medium-holed crumb tells you the rise, hydration and fry temperature all landed correctly.

Old-School Sweet Curry Mince

1
Soften the onion
Heat the 30 ml macadamia oil (from Prep) in the pan over medium heat (roughly 150°C) and fry the chopped onion (from Prep) until soft and translucent — no spices yet. Add the crushed garlic (from Prep) for the final 30 seconds.
2
Loosen the pan
Add the splash of water (about 30 ml, from Prep), stirring to lift anything stuck to the base. Makes the mince easier to break up once it goes in.
3
Brown the mince — lightly
Medium-high heat (roughly 180°C). Add the 500 g beef mince (from Prep) and break it up as it cooks. Just lose the raw colour, lightly coloured all over — not deeply browned. It builds full flavour during the simmer ahead.
4
Build the sauce base Taste
Add the 6 g salt, ~0.5 g white pepper, 15 ml Worcestershire sauce and the tin (400 g) chopped tomatoes (all from Prep). Stir through thoroughly, then taste — this is your base flavour before any spice or acid.
5
Add the spices Taste
Stir in the 15 g curry powder, 4 g coriander, 0.5 g nutmeg and 1 g five spice (from Prep) — hold back the turmeric. Cook a minute or two, then taste again: nutmeg and five spice are both potent and easy to overshoot. Adjust, then simmer gently 15–20 minutes total until tender.
6
Add the vinegar Taste
Stir in the 15 ml brown vinegar (from Prep), simmer a minute or two, then taste a third time — this shows the sweet-versus-acid balance before any sweetness goes in.
7
Sweeten
Stir in the 60 g apricot chutney and 40 g apricot jam (from Prep). Simmer a further 5 minutes to let the sweetness settle in.
8
Add the turmeric — late
In the final 2–3 minutes, stir in the 1 g turmeric set aside in Prep — keeps its colour bright and its flavour clean rather than dulled by a long simmer.
9
Thicken
Stir in the gravy paste mixed in Prep (15 g Bisto gravy powder + 30 ml water), simmer 2–3 minutes to a thick, scoopable consistency — not runny, not so dry it falls apart when spooned.

To Serve

✓
Fill and serve
Slice each vetkoek open, not all the way through, and fill generously with the warm sweet curry mince.

Flavour Wheel

Balancing the Flavour

Three tasting points are built into the method — after the base, after the spices, after the vinegar — so you can catch and correct the balance before it's locked in by the sweetness and thickener.

The core rule: every sweet element needs an acid to answer it. Chutney and jam bring sweetness; brown vinegar cuts through it so the dish doesn't taste flat or cloying. Too sweet → more vinegar. Too sharp → a touch more chutney or jam.

Salt sharpens every other flavour — a muted mince is often missing salt, not another ingredient. White pepper sits with the salt for background warmth, without black pepper's more forward, grassy heat or dark flecks.

Umami from the browned mince, tomatoes and gravy powder gives body and richness — part of why the mince is only lightly browned early, since its full savoury depth builds during the simmer.

Aromatic warmth (curry powder, five spice, nutmeg, coriander) should stay in the background. One spice jumping out usually means it went in too early or too heavy-handed relative to the others.

Tips

Notes on Technique

Liquid no hotter than 35–36°C: yeast weakens well before boiling point, with danger territory from the mid-40s°C and full kill-off around 60°C. 35–36°C, close to body temperature, sits safely inside the active range — warm, not hot, to the touch.

Oil never below 140°C: below that, the crust takes too long to set and seal, so the dough absorbs noticeably more oil before it's sealed. Work in the 165–170°C range and treat 140°C strictly as a floor.

Three-times sifting: breaks up lumps and spreads the salt and sugar evenly through the flour, for more even fermentation.

Light-browning the mince: lean mince in small pieces dries out and toughens fast under high heat. It simmers for 15–20 minutes afterward anyway, so there's no need to drive off extra moisture upfront.

The black plastic bag: keeps dough warmer than the surrounding air on a cool day. On a hot, sunny day it can run past the target temperature — check on it rather than leaving it for a long stretch.

Baked Sago Pudding with Apricot Jam & Meringue

Yield: 6 portions ★★★★☆ 4/5 Ease 6/10

Equipment

Prep — Mise en Place

Get all of this ready before you start cooking. It isn’t repeated in the steps below — they just refer back to “from Prep”.

Get Ready

1
Soak the sago
Rinse 190 g sago pearls under cold water, then cover with cold water and soak for 1 hour. Drain well through a fine sieve. Don’t skip this — unsoaked sago cooks unevenly, with a harder centre in some pearls. Start this first — everything else can happen while it soaks.
2
Weigh the custard base
1000 ml (1 litre) full-cream milk, 50 g white sugar, 55 g salted butter (cubed), 1.5 g salt — measured out and ready by the stove.
3
Separate the eggs
4 large eggs: yolks into a bowl with the 5 ml vanilla extract whisked in; whites into a separate, grease-free bowl, reserved for the meringue.
4
Butter the dish and stage the bain-marie
Butter 1 large oven dish (or 6 individual ramekins). Have the roasting pan and a kettle of water ready for the bain-marie at bake time.
5
Preheat the oven
Preheat to 160°C ahead of the first bake.
6
Weigh the jam & meringue topping
180 g apricot jam, 10 g cornflour, 2 g cream of tartar, 200 g caster sugar.

Cooking Instructions

Sago Custard Base

1
Build the milk base
Warm the milk (from Prep) in the saucepan over medium heat. Add the sugar, butter and salt (from Prep), stirring until the butter melts and the sugar dissolves. Bring to a gentle simmer — not a rolling boil.
2
Cook the sago until fully translucent Check
Stir in the drained sago (from Prep). Reduce to low heat and simmer gently, stirring regularly so it doesn’t catch on the base of the pot, until every pearl has turned from opaque white to fully translucent — usually 15–20 minutes. Bite one: there should be no hard white speck at the centre. This takes longer than feels natural; rushing it at higher heat scorches the pot without finishing the pearls.
3
Temper in the egg yolks
Remove from the heat and cool for about 5 minutes. Take the egg yolks prepped with vanilla (from Prep), then whisk in a ladleful of the warm sago mixture to bring them up to temperature gradually. Stir this tempered mixture back into the main pot, off the heat, until fully combined.
4
First bake — set the custard Check
Pour into the buttered dish (from Prep) and set inside the roasting pan. Pour hot water from the kettle into the roasting pan until it comes about halfway up the sides of the pudding dish. Bake at the preheated 160°C for 30 minutes (large dish) or about 20 minutes (ramekins), until set at the edges but still gently wobbling across the centre — not sunken-liquid, not fully firm. It keeps setting from residual heat and through the second bake, so pulling it here rather than at fully firm is what protects it from turning rubbery.

Jam, Meringue & Second Bake

5
Warm and spread the jam
While the base bakes, warm the 180 g apricot jam (from Prep) slightly — a few seconds in the microwave or a small pot — just until it loosens enough to spread easily. Don’t let it boil. Spread evenly over the warm baked custard.
6
Dust with cornflour
Very lightly dust the jam layer with the 10 g cornflour (from Prep) through a small sieve. This thin, barely-visible layer stops the meringue from weeping syrup where it sits against the moist jam, and helps it hold its shape during the second bake.
7
Whip the meringue
Whisk the reserved egg whites (from Prep) with the 2 g cream of tartar (from Prep) until foamy, then add the 200 g caster sugar (from Prep) gradually, a spoonful at a time, whisking continuously until stiff, glossy, and holding a firm peak on the whisk.
8
Top and seal
Pile or pipe the meringue over the jam layer, spreading it right to the edges of the dish so it seals the jam layer completely. Any exposed jam will bubble and scorch in the second bake.
9
Second bake — set the meringue Check
Return to the 160°C oven (no water bath needed) for 15–20 minutes, until the meringue peaks are golden brown and set on the outside but still soft within. A cook’s blowtorch over the piped peaks is a faster alternative if you’d rather not run the oven again — good for ramekins plated to order.

What a properly finished bake looks like

Meringue peaks deep golden brown, crisp outside and soft within. Custard set at the edges with a gentle give in the centre when spooned — not runny, not rubbery.

Rest & Serve

10
Rest before serving
Let the pudding stand for 5–10 minutes out of the oven before serving. This lets the custard finish settling so it holds together cleanly when spooned, rather than running.
11
To serve
Serve warm, spooned straight from the dish. For individually plated service, torch the meringue on each ramekin to order and serve immediately while the meringue is still warm and the custard beneath is soft.

Flavour Wheel

Balancing the Flavour

Every rich, milky element here needs a tart note to answer it, or the dish reads as flat and one-note — the same principle as the curry mince. The apricot jam’s tartness is what cuts through the sweetness of the custard and meringue; without it, three layers of sweet-on-sweet-on-sweet turns cloying fast. If the finished pudding tastes flat, it’s rarely a sugar problem — usually the jam layer is too thin, or too sweet a jam was used instead of a properly tart apricot one.

Salt in the custard isn’t there to taste salty — at 1.5 g it sharpens the milk and vanilla underneath the sugar. Skipping it makes the custard taste duller, not just less salty.

Vanilla should sit in the background, rounding out the milk flavour rather than announcing itself. If it’s noticeable as its own flavour, it’s been overdosed.

Texture matters as much as taste: soft custard, a thin jammy layer, and a crisp-then-soft meringue crust are three different textures in one spoonful. Losing any one of the three — an unset custard, a jam layer melted into the custard, or an over-baked, chalky meringue — flattens that contrast.

Tips

Notes on Technique

Weigh the sago and other measured ingredients in grams: a cup or ml of sago, jam or sugar packs differently depending how firmly it’s scooped or how warm the jam is. Weighing removes that variable.

The sago must go fully translucent before anything else: an opaque centre means unhydrated starch that will never fully soften in the oven bake that follows. This has to be solved on the stovetop, not in the oven.

Temper the yolks, don’t stir them straight in: egg yolks scramble around 70–80°C. Warming them gradually with a ladleful of the hot mixture first lets them thicken the custard instead of curdling into visible flecks.

The bain-marie for the first bake: direct oven heat cooks the outside of an egg-thickened custard much faster than the centre, giving a rubbery edge and a curdled texture. The water bath moderates the heat so the whole custard sets gently and evenly.

Pull it at “still wobbling gently”, not fully firm: the custard keeps cooking from residual heat once out of the oven, and again through the second bake. Baking to fully firm at this stage all but guarantees an overcooked, rubbery result by the end.

Dust the jam with cornflour before the meringue: meringue piped onto a moist, sugary surface tends to weep syrup underneath as it bakes, or slide off. A thin cornflour dusting absorbs that surface moisture and gives the meringue something drier to grip.

Cream of tartar in the meringue: a mild acid that stabilises the whipped egg-white proteins, giving a meringue that holds stiff peaks longer and is less likely to weep or deflate before it reaches the oven.

Add the caster sugar gradually: whipping it in a spoonful at a time lets it fully dissolve into the whites as they whip, giving a glossy, stable meringue rather than a grainy one that weeps sugar syrup during baking.
